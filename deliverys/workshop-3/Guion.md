## Slide 1 — Portada · **Bruno** (≈0:45)

InfoCar is B2B: we sell to used-car dealers in Chile, with a second line for private buyers. This deck builds on the last one: context, objectives and method stay, with your feedback applied. Today's new part is population and sample: who we will ask, and how many.

---

## Slide 14 — The story so far · **Seba** (≈1:00)

Before the new part, the story so far in one slide. The market is big: over a million used cars changed hands in 2025, and 85 percent were bought by people. Our first customer is the dealer: there are 487 small and medium dealers in Santiago, and each buys about three cars a month. The problem is information asymmetry: the buyer cannot see the car's condition, used cars have no legal warranty, and fraud cases are rising. Our answer is a condition report from phone photos, paid monthly by dealers or per report by private buyers. And our objective is to understand what certainty is worth, listening first and then measuring. Now: who we will ask, and how many.

## Slide 15 — Who we will ask, and how many · **Gabo** (≈0:45)

So far we said what we want to understand and how: listen first, then measure. Today we close the chain: who we will ask, and how many cases we need. We followed five rules from class and checked them against the literature. We describe each population with five dimensions. A rule that applies to everyone is a filter, not a segment. Each cell needs at least 30 cases, and prices and totals are read on bigger bases. In stage 1 we look for different profiles, not big numbers. And we show the ideal design first and then the realistic one, because only a census covers everyone.

## Slide 16 — B2B population: dealers who buy to resell · **Joaco** (≈1:00)

Our first population is B2B, because the dealer pays every month. The tax office publishes a list of firms with their activity, size and comuna, so we can count them instead of guessing. We take code 451002, retail sale of vehicles, which includes compraventas: 4,116 firms in Chile, 1,066 small or medium, and 487 of those in Santiago, each with a name and tax ID. We only work in Santiago. We split the 487 by the tax office's own sales brackets: 255 small firms that buy about one car a month, 136 that buy about three, and 96 mid-size firms that buy about nine. The informant is whoever sets the purchase price.

---

## Slide 17 — B2C population: private buyers · **Checho** (≈1:00)

The second population is private buyers in Santiago. Five filters apply to everyone: 18 or older, living in Santiago, a used-car purchase in the last 12 months or planned in the next 6, own money, and a part in the decision. Two variables split the sample. Seller type: from a company, consumer law applies; from a private person, it does not. And purchase timing: people who already bought tell us what really happened; people about to buy tell us what they need before paying. Age, sex, income and the value of the car are recorded, not used as quotas. That is about 485,000 used cars, SUVs and pickups bought by people in Santiago each year.

## Slide 19 — We group cars by the value of their permit · **Dani** (≈1:10)

In class you asked us to make our vehicle groups explicit. We use the value of the circulation permit: every car pays it, and the law sets it from the tax office appraisal before any sale, so it is objective and public. We took the official table, 65,812 versions, and weighted it with real transfers. Equal thirds fail, because 28 percent of cars pay the legal minimum. So we used Dalenius–Hodges, the classic rule for strata of a skewed variable, and a second method agreed. The cuts are 100 and 200 monthly tax units of appraisal: low, mid and high value, 56, 28 and 16 percent of the market. To classify a car we only need brand, model and year.

## Slide 20 — Stage 1: diversity, not numbers · **Vicho** (≈1:00)

This is the ideal design for stage 1. For dealers we use five profiles and two interviews per profile, so ten, plus two in reserve: we stop when two interviews in a row bring nothing new. The two interviews of a profile are different on purpose, for example one dealer with low-value stock and one with mid-value. Profile five is a contrast, not a customer. For buyers we cross seller type with purchase timing: four focus groups, so we hear people who already bought and people about to buy, from private sellers and from companies.

---

## Slide 21 — Ideal: 100 dealer surveys in three cells · **Gabo** (≈1:00)

In the ideal design we survey 100 of the 487 dealers in Santiago, drawn at random from the tax office list inside each cell. How many per cell? A proportional split would give 52, 28 and 20, but every cell needs at least 30 cases, so the two smaller cells go up to 30 and the other 40 go to the largest one. Weights bring the totals back to the real mix: the reference error is plus or minus 9.1 percent. Recent studies show firms answer between 34 and 55 percent of surveys, so we contact between 180 and 290 firms, almost all of the 96 mid-size ones. In the choice experiment each dealer prices three cars, one per value segment, blind or with the repair cost.

---

## Slide 22 — Ideal: 300 buyer surveys in four cells · **Seba** (≈1:00)

For buyers, the ideal design crosses seller type with purchase timing: four cells of 75, 300 in total. Why 75? It is well above the 30-case floor, so each seller type and each timing reads at plus or minus 8 percent, and prices are read on each seller type. People about to buy tell us the seller type they expect. Most answers come from an online panel with quotas, and up to 80 from intercepts at notaries, analysed apart. Car value is not a quota: we record it and weight to the market mix.

---

## Slide 23 — Ideal vs. realistic: what fits in two weeks · **Bruno** (≈1:00)

Why are the last three slides the ideal design? Because they meet our precision targets: at least 30 cases in every cell, and totals near plus or minus 10 percent or better, with no limit of time or money. The realistic design is what seven of us can run in two weeks in Santiago without a paid panel: 60 dealers and 180 buyers, eight interviews and two online focus groups. The right column says what we lose: precision, the franchise contrast, and the timing split inside the focus groups.

At the bottom, the two designs: ideal, 100 dealers and 300 buyers; realistic, 60 and 180.
